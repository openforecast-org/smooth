\title{The likelihood of intermittent demand and of the unobserved periods}
\author{Technical report on the \code{smooth} package (R and Python)}
\date{October 2026}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
This report derives the likelihood that \code{adam()}, \code{tbats()}, \code{sm()} and the
other functions of \code{smooth} use when some sizes are not observed: the zeros of an
occurrence model and the missing values. It explains why the differential entropy of the zeros
was removed (Section~\ref{sec:entropy}), how the variance of the error after the unobserved
periods enters the likelihood of the pure additive models (Section~\ref{sec:additive}) and of
the pure multiplicative ADAM ETS (Section~\ref{sec:multiplicative}), how the same quantities
give the prediction intervals (Section~\ref{sec:intervals}), what remains approximate or not
implemented (Sections~\ref{sec:others} and~\ref{sec:gain}), and where the code is
(Section~\ref{sec:implementation}). The notation follows \citet{iETS}, written as
Svetunkov \& Boylan (2023) below, and the ADAM monograph \citep{ADAM}.
\end{abstract}

\section{The model}

The demand $y_t$ is the product of the occurrence $o_t$ and of the potential demand size $z_t$,
which are independent (assumption 3 of Svetunkov \& Boylan, 2023):
\begin{equation}
y_t = o_t z_t, \qquad o_t \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p_t),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
z_t = w(\vect v_{t-\vect l}) + r(\vect v_{t-\vect l})\,\epsilon_t, \qquad
\vect v_t = f(\vect v_{t-\vect l}) + g(\vect v_{t-\vect l})\,\epsilon_t .
\end{equation}
For iETS(M,N,N), the case of the paper, $z_t = l_{t-1}(1+\epsilon_t)$ and
$l_t = l_{t-1}(1+\alpha\epsilon_t)$.

$T$ is the number of observations, $T_1 = \sum_t o_t$ the number of the observed sizes and
$T_0 = T - T_1$ the number of the zeros. $j_t$ is the number of periods since the previous
observed size (the demand interval): $j_t = 1$ when $y_{t-1}$ was observed, and the first gap
counts from the initial states. A missing value is an unobserved period too: everything below
holds for the missing values as for the zeros, without the occurrence part.

\paragraph{The estimation.} In the paper's Appendix C.1, and in the C++ fitter of
\code{smooth}, the states are updated only when the size is observed: at a zero (or a missing
value) the error is set to zero and the states go on with the transition,
$\hat{\vect v}_t = f(\hat{\vect v}_{t-\vect l})$. The fitted value at the next observed size is
the prediction $j_t$ steps ahead from the states at the previous one, $\hat\mu_t$.

\section{The likelihood of the observed data}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The distribution of $y_t$}{The distribution of y\_t}}

$y_t$ is a mixture of a point mass at zero and a continuous density. Since $z_t$ is continuous,
$P(z_t = 0) = 0$, and a zero can only come from $o_t = 0$:
\begin{equation}
P(y_t = 0 \mid Y_{t-1}) = (1-p_t)\int_0^\infty f_z(z \mid Y_{t-1})\,dz = 1 - p_t,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
g(y_t \mid Y_{t-1}) = p_t\, f_z(y_t \mid Y_{t-1}) \quad \text{for } y_t > 0 .
\end{equation}
The size of a zero is integrated out, and the integral of a density is one: nothing about the
distribution of the size is observed at a zero, so the size cannot contribute there.

\subsection{The likelihood of the sample}

The likelihood is the product of the conditional distributions of what is observed (the
prediction-error decomposition). The paper's (B.3) is the \emph{complete-data} likelihood
$L_c(\theta,\lambda \mid Y, Z_0)$, with $Z_0 = \{z_t : o_t = 0\}$ the unobserved sizes; the
observed-data likelihood integrates them out:
\begin{equation}
L(\theta,\lambda \mid Y) = \int L_c(\theta,\lambda \mid Y, Z_0)\, dZ_0
= \prod_{o_t=1} p_t \prod_{o_t=0}(1-p_t) \int \prod_{t=1}^T f_z(z_t \mid \vect v_{t-1})\, dZ_0 .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{The exact model.} The states between the demands depend on the unobserved sizes.
Integrating them out one after the other gives, at each observed size, the $j_t$-steps ahead
density from the states at the previous one (the paper's B.4):
\begin{equation}
\ell(\theta,\lambda \mid Y) = \sum_{o_t=1} \log f_z^{(j_t)}\!\left(z_t \mid \vect v_{t-j_t}\right)
+ \sum_{o_t=1}\log p_t + \sum_{o_t=0}\log(1-p_t).
\label{eq:exact}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{With the one-step density.} This is what \code{smooth} did until the changes of
Section~\ref{sec:gaps}, and still does for the models of Section~\ref{sec:others}:
\begin{equation}
\ell_b(\theta,\lambda \mid Y) = \sum_{o_t=1} \log f_z\!\left(z_t \mid \hat\mu_t\right)
+ \sum_{o_t=1}\log \hat p_t + \sum_{o_t=0}\log(1-\hat p_t).
\label{eq:onestep}
\end{equation}

In neither is there a term for the sizes at the zeros: the zeros enter through
$\log(1-\hat p_t)$, and through $j_t$ in the exact one. The two parts separate, so the
occurrence model is estimated on its own, as the paper notes.

\section{Why the differential entropy was removed}
\label{sec:entropy}

\subsection{The step from (B.5) to (B.6)}

The EM algorithm \citep{EM} takes the expectation of the complete-data log-likelihood under
the \emph{current} parameters $\theta^{(k)}$ and maximises it over $\theta$, with
$\theta^{(k)}$ fixed:
\begin{equation}
Q\!\left(\theta \mid \theta^{(k)}\right) = \sum_{o_t=1}\log f_z(z_t \mid \theta)
+ \sum_{o_t=0} \int f_z\!\left(z \mid \theta^{(k)}\right) \log f_z(z \mid \theta)\, dz
+ \sum_{o_t=1}\log p_t + \sum_{o_t=0}\log(1-p_t).
\end{equation}
This is (B.5) with its two parameter vectors written out. The term of each zero is a
cross-entropy,
\begin{equation}
\int f_z^{(k)} \log f_z\, dz = -H_z\!\left(\theta^{(k)}\right)
- \KL\!\left(f_z^{(k)} \,\middle\|\, f_z\right),
\end{equation}
equal to $-H_z(\theta)$ only at $\theta = \theta^{(k)}$. (B.6) writes $-H_z(\theta)$, which sets
$\theta^{(k)} = \theta$ \emph{before} the maximisation: the distribution of the missing sizes
then moves with the parameters, and the objective is no longer that of EM.

At $\theta = \theta^{(k)}$ the gradient of the term of a zero is the mean of the score, which is
zero:
\begin{equation}
\int f_z \,\frac{\partial \log f_z}{\partial \theta}\, dz
= \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta}\int f_z\, dz = 0 .
\end{equation}
So $\nabla Q = \nabla \ell_b$ at every step, and the fixed points of EM are the stationary
points of $\ell_b$ \citep{EM}. The gradient of $-H_z(\theta)$ is not zero, and that is the bias.

A separate remark on the paper: with $H = -\mathrm{E}(\log f)$, (29) and (B.6) should read
$-\sum H_z(z_t)$; the code had the minus sign.

\subsection{The consequences}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The scale is shrunk by the share of the non-zero observations.} For the normal
distribution, EM iterates
$\sigma^2_{(k+1)} = \bigl(\sum_{o_t=1} e_t^2 + T_0\sigma^2_{(k)}\bigr)/T$, whose fixed point is
$\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{T_1}\sum_{o_t=1} e_t^2$, while (B.6) gives
$\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{o_t=1} e_t^2$. On ETS(A,N,N) with $\sigma^2 = 25$ and $p = 0.4$
(persistence and initial level fixed at their true values, 40 series of 600 observations),
\code{adam()} gave 11.26 and the scale of the observed sizes 28.13: a ratio of exactly 0.40.
\item \textbf{The entropy can pull the location.} For the log-normal iETS(M,N,N),
$-H_z = -\log \hat l_{t-1} + \frac{\sigma^2}{2} - \frac12\log(2\pi e\sigma^2)$ grows without
bound as $\hat l_{t-1}\to 0$; for the gamma and the inverse Gaussian distributions the entropy
contains the fitted value too.
\item \textbf{The likelihood is unbounded with a dynamic scale.} With a scale model
(\code{sm()}), the zeros alone reward a scale going to zero (or to infinity for the gamma):
\code{sm()} of a gamma model with an occurrence model diverged, with a loss of
$-9.1\times10^{16}$.
\item \textbf{The selection of the distribution is biased}: \code{tbats(distribution="auto")}
chose the S distribution instead of the normal on log-normal sizes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What changed}

The zeros have only $\log(1-\hat p_t)$ in the likelihood of \code{adam()}, \code{tbats()} and
\code{sm()} (and in \code{pointLik()}), and the scale is the maximum likelihood estimate over
the observed sizes, divided by $T_1$; its de-biasing uses $T_1$ less the parameters. The scale
models of \code{sm()} treat the zeros as missing values of their response, so that their states
go on through them and their fitted values are the scale (they were the scale times the
probability).

\section{The error after the unobserved periods}
\label{sec:gaps}

After $j_t - 1$ unobserved periods, the states have gone on with zero errors while the true
ones received $j_t - 1$ errors. The error at the next observed size is therefore the error
$j_t$ steps ahead, not the one-step one, and its distribution is wider. Its first two moments
are exact for the two classes below, because the drift of the states over the gap is linear in
the errors.

\subsection{Pure additive models: the normal distribution, exactly}
\label{sec:additive}

For a pure additive model ($\vect F$, $\vect w$ and $\vect g$ constant, which covers ETS with
additive components, ARIMA, the regressors, CES, GUM, SSARIMA, SPARMA and TBATS in the Box-Cox
space), the error at the next observed size is
\begin{equation}
e_t = \epsilon_t + \sum_{i=1}^{j_t-1} c_i\,\epsilon_{t-i}, \qquad
c_i = \vect w'\vect F^{i-1}\vect g ,
\end{equation}
with the coefficients $c_i$ of the lags of each component (those of \code{covarAnal()}, used
by the analytical intervals). Its mean is zero and its variance is $\sigma^2\kappa_t$ with
\begin{equation}
\kappa_t = 1 + \sum_{i=1}^{j_t-1} c_i^2 .
\label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
With the normal distribution, $e_t \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2\kappa_t)$ exactly, so
\begin{equation}
\ell = \sum_{o_t=1} \log \phi\!\left(e_t;\, 0,\, \sigma^2\kappa_t\right)
+ \text{occurrence terms}, \qquad
\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{T_1}\sum_{o_t=1} \frac{e_t^2}{\kappa_t}.
\end{equation}
The standardised residuals divide each error by $\sqrt{\kappa_t}$. On a series of 300
observations of ETS(A,N,N) with $\sigma^2 = 25$, $\alpha = 0.3$ and $p = 0.4$, the estimates
are $\hat\sigma^2 = 25.7$ and $\hat\alpha = 0.32$. With the one-step density, the scale of the
observed sizes in the simulation of Section~\ref{sec:entropy} was 28.1 for a true 25: the
errors after the gaps inflate it.

\subsection{Pure multiplicative ADAM ETS: the log-normal distribution, exactly}
\label{sec:multiplicative}

The ADAM form of ETS (\code{ets="adam"}) updates a multiplicative state by a power of
$1+\epsilon_t$: $l_t = l_{t-1}b_{t-1}^{\phi}(1+\epsilon_t)^{\alpha}$,
$b_t = b_{t-1}^{\phi}(1+\epsilon_t)^{\beta}$ and $s_t = s_{t-m}(1+\epsilon_t)^{\gamma}$. With
$u_t = \log(1+\epsilon_t)$, a model with all the components multiplicative (or none) is exactly
linear in logs,
\begin{equation}
\log z_t = \vect w'\log\vect v_{t-\vect l} + u_t, \qquad
\log\vect v_t = \vect F\log\vect v_{t-\vect l} + \vect g\,u_t ,
\end{equation}
with the same $\vect F$, $\vect w$ and $\vect g$ as the additive model:
Section~\ref{sec:additive} applies to the logarithms, with the error $u_t$. The drift over a gap
is $\sum_{i<j_t} c_i u_{t-i}$, and since the mean of $u_t$ is not zero it shifts the location:
\begin{equation}
\log\frac{z_t}{\hat\mu_t} = u_t + \sum_{i=1}^{j_t-1} c_i\,u_{t-i}, \qquad
a_t = 1 + \sum_{i=1}^{j_t-1} c_i .
\label{eq:a}
\end{equation}
With the log-normal distribution, $u_t \sim \mathcal N(-s/2,\, s)$, so that $1+\epsilon_t$
has the mean of one, and the sum is normal:
\begin{equation}
\log\frac{z_t}{\hat\mu_t} \sim \mathcal N\!\left(-\frac{s}{2}a_t,\; s\,\kappa_t\right),
\label{eq:lognormalgap}
\end{equation}
an exact density, with no approximation. The conventional form ($1+\alpha\epsilon_t$) is not
linear in $u_t$, which is why this section is for \code{ets="adam"} only.

\paragraph{The scale.} With $u_t = \log(z_t/\hat\mu_t)$,
\begin{equation}
\ell(s) = -\frac12\sum_{o_t=1}\log(s\kappa_t)
- \sum_{o_t=1}\frac{\left(u_t + s a_t/2\right)^2}{2 s\kappa_t} + \dots,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial s} = -\frac{T_1}{2s}
+ \frac{1}{2s^2}\sum_{o_t=1}\frac{u_t^2}{\kappa_t}
- \frac18\sum_{o_t=1}\frac{a_t^2}{\kappa_t} = 0
\;\Longleftrightarrow\;
\left(\sum_{o_t=1}\frac{a_t^2}{\kappa_t}\right)s^2 + 4T_1 s
- 4\sum_{o_t=1}\frac{u_t^2}{\kappa_t} = 0,
\end{equation}
whose positive root is the maximum likelihood estimate:
\begin{equation}
\hat s = \frac{2\left(\sqrt{T_1^2 + \sum_{o_t=1}\frac{a_t^2}{\kappa_t}
\sum_{o_t=1}\frac{u_t^2}{\kappa_t}} - T_1\right)}{\sum_{o_t=1} a_t^2/\kappa_t} .
\end{equation}
Without gaps ($a_t = \kappa_t = 1$) this is $\hat s = 2\left(\sqrt{1+m}-1\right)$ with
$m = \frac{1}{T_1}\sum u_t^2$. \code{smooth} had $2\left(1-\sqrt{1-m}\right)$, the root of
$s^2 - 4s + 4m = 0$, which is not the maximum of the likelihood: with $s = 0.5$ it gave 0.684
against 0.503 (and 0.503 by direct optimisation). It is fixed for all the log-normal models.

The residuals of the diagnostics are mapped to the one-step ones,
$\exp\!\left(\frac{u_t + s a_t/2}{\sqrt{\kappa_t}} - \frac s2\right)$, which are
$\log\mathcal N(-s/2, s)$ again.

On ADAM ETS(M,N,N) with $s = 0.2$, $\alpha = 0.3$ and $p = 0.35$ (30 series of 300
observations), the mean estimates are $\hat s = 0.205$ and $\hat\alpha = 0.431$, against
$\hat s = 0.324$ and $\hat\alpha = 0.483$ before.

\subsection{The prediction intervals}
\label{sec:intervals}

A forecast $h$ steps ahead is a gap of $h$ periods, so the quantities above give the analytical
intervals too.

\paragraph{The log-normal.} The scale $s$ is the variance of $\log(1+\epsilon_t)$, not of
$\epsilon_t$, which is $e^{s}-1$. The one-step interval of $1+\epsilon_t$ is therefore given by
the quantiles of $\log\mathcal N(-s/2, s)$. For the pure multiplicative ADAM ETS the $h$-step one
is, by~\eqref{eq:lognormalgap}, exactly
\begin{equation}
\frac{z_{t+h}}{\hat\mu_{t+h}} \sim \log\mathcal N\!\left(-\frac{s}{2}a_h,\; s\,\kappa_h\right),
\end{equation}
with $a_h$ and $\kappa_h$ of~\eqref{eq:kappa} and~\eqref{eq:a} for $j = h$, around the point
forecast $\hat\mu_{t+h}$ (the forecast with zero errors). For the conventional ETS the variance
of the multiplicative error of \code{adamVarAnal()} is computed from $e^{s}-1$, and the
variance $V_h$ of the logarithm is $\log(1+\mathrm{that})$; the interval is then that of
$\log\mathcal N(-V_h/2, V_h)$, exact at $h=1$ and an approximation after. Before, the one-step
fitted intervals of \code{predict()} mapped $s$ through $2(1-\sqrt{1-s})$ once more, and the
forecast intervals took $\sqrt{|1-V_h|}-1$ as the mean of the logarithm and $s$ as the variance
of the error: with $s=0.5$ the one-step 90\% bounds relative to the forecast were 0.27 and 2.29
(and 0.25 and 3.30 for the fitted values), against the true 0.24 and 2.52.

\paragraph{The occurrence.} The analytical intervals of a model with an occurrence model are
those of the sizes at the level $(\text{level} - (1-p_t))/p_t$, so they are around the forecast
(or the fitted value) of the sizes, $\hat\mu_t$, and not around the forecast of the demand,
$p_t\hat\mu_t$. Before, they were around the latter: with $p = 0.42$ the upper bound of the 90\%
interval was $0.64\hat\mu$, against $1.53\hat\mu$ for the simulated one.

\subsection{The other models: not implemented}
\label{sec:others}

For the other distributions and for the conventional multiplicative ETS, the error after a gap
is the exact one-step error times a drift that is a sum of $j_t - 1$ independent terms,
\begin{equation}
\frac{z_t}{\hat\mu_t} = (1+\epsilon_t)\,e^{S_t}, \qquad
S_t = \sum_{i=1}^{j_t-1}\log\left(1+\alpha\epsilon_{t-i}\right)
\end{equation}
(for iETS(M,N,N)). The exact density of $S_t$ has no closed form (a product of gammas is not a
gamma), but its mean and variance do, or come from a quadrature once per evaluation of the
likelihood. Taking $S_t$ normal and mixing the exact one-step density over it with
Gauss-Hermite nodes,
\begin{equation}
\tilde f_z(z_t) = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_k\, f_z\!\left(z_t \mid \hat\mu_t e^{d_k}\right),
\qquad d_k = m_{j_t} + \sqrt{2v_{j_t}}\,x_k ,
\end{equation}
is exact at $j_t = 1$. Table~\ref{tab:kl} compares it with the other approximations against
the exact density of the gap, computed by FFT convolution on a grid of the gamma, log-normal
and inverse Gaussian distributions ($\sigma^2$ from 0.05 to 1, $\alpha$ from 0.1 to 0.6, $j$
from 2 to 20).

The mixed models (additive and multiplicative components together) are linear in neither
space; their moments would need the propagation of the covariance of the states through the
gap, a recursion which was left aside.

\section{The smoothing parameters after the gaps}
\label{sec:gain}

With the likelihood of Section~\ref{sec:gaps}, the scale is unbiased, but the smoothing
parameters are still biased upwards (the paper's Appendix C). The cause is in the update of the
states, not in the likelihood: after a gap, the level is updated by $\alpha$ times an error that
contains the whole drift of the gap, while the true level moved by all of it. In logs
(Section~\ref{sec:multiplicative}, ETS(M,N,N)), the change of the level since the last demand
and the error at the observed size are
\begin{equation}
\log\frac{l_t}{l_{t-j}} = S_t + \alpha u_t, \qquad \log\frac{z_t}{l_{t-j}} = S_t + u_t,
\end{equation}
with $\Var(S_t) = (j-1)\alpha^2 s$, so the regression of the first on the second has the gain
\begin{equation}
\alpha_j = \frac{(j-1)\alpha^2 + \alpha}{(j-1)\alpha^2 + 1},
\end{equation}
which is $\alpha$ at $j = 1$ and tends to 1 as the gap grows. Table~\ref{tab:gain} shows its
effect on iETS(M,N,N) with gamma errors.
